Gravity, and enough time: how galaxies pull each other apart

Two galaxies pass close by. Neither explodes, and not one star strikes another —
yet both are drawn out into streamers of light a hundred thousand light years
long. This film builds that result from nothing but gravity, and then follows it
to its conclusion: what becomes of spiral galaxies that meet.

Everything you see is simulated in a web browser, in real time, on a single
desktop machine. You can run any of it yourself at the link below.

WHAT IS BEING SIMULATED

Two galaxies, each a bulge, a disc and a dark-matter halo. The discs are made of
particles that feel each other's gravity, so the spiral arms in the opening are
not drawn in — they grow, on their own, out of a smooth disc. Nothing else is at
work: no magnetic fields, no explosions, no collisions between stars.

The stars really do never touch. A galaxy is overwhelmingly empty; two of them
can pass straight through one another and the odds of any two stars colliding are
negligible. What tears the discs apart is tidal force alone.


THE RESULT WORTH POINTING AT

Which way a disc spins turns out to matter enormously.

Run the same encounter twice, changing nothing but the direction the discs
rotate. Turning WITH the encounter, a galaxy throws 15.1 per cent of its disc
beyond 20 kiloparsecs. Turning AGAINST it, 2.5 per cent. The two orbits are
asserted identical to one part in a million first, so the spin is demonstrably
the only difference.

Put another way: reversing the spin spares a galaxy as much as moving the whole
encounter from 25 to 46.6 kiloparsecs — nearly twice as far away.

That exchange rate is measured, not asserted. An earlier version of this project
claimed spin "matters more than how close the encounter is" without ever having
varied the distance, and that claim has been retracted.


WHAT THE MERGER SHOWS

When two galaxies cannot escape one another, the orbit decays and they coalesce.
The remnant is measured against the disc it came from: ordered rotation falls
from 205 km/s to 27, while random motion rises from 29 to 149. A
rotation-supported disc has become a pressure-supported spheroid, which is the
defining difference between a spiral and an elliptical.

The light profile moves the same way, from an exponential towards a de
Vaucouleurs law — but only when the dark-matter halo is made of particles too and
is free to respond. With a rigid halo it does not happen at all. Violent
relaxation needs a potential that changes with time.

It reaches a Sérsic index of 2.58, not the 4 of a classical bright elliptical, so
the honest word is "towards". There is no gas in this model, and one merger is
not how a real elliptical is assembled.


ON THE MILKY WAY AND ANDROMEDA

For years the answer was "they will certainly merge, in about four billion
years". With Gaia and Hubble astrometry, and observational uncertainties included
for the first time, that certainty has gone: the current estimate is close to
even odds over the next ten billion years. The confident version came from
ignoring the error bars.


HOW IT IS MADE

WebGPU compute shaders, plain ES modules, no build step. Direct N-body
self-gravity for the discs and halos; a leapfrog integrator that is exactly
time-reversible in double precision; dynamical friction that emerges from the
particles rather than being added as a formula.

The film is rendered offline at 4K60 with sixteen sub-frame samples per frame for
true motion blur, then cut, scored and narrated from the same timeline, so the
subtitles cannot drift against the picture.

The physics carries 98 standing assertions that run on every build, including
checks designed to FAIL when the code is broken on purpose. Where a number
appears in this description, a test reads it back out of the prose and fails the
build if it has drifted.
